My research, explained simply

Everything around you, from your phone battery to the leaves on a tree, is made of atoms held together by electrons. I use computers and the laws of quantum physics to watch atoms and electrons move, so we can design better materials for clean energy. Here is what that means, one animation at a time.

My journey so far

The tools: how I watch atoms and electrons

Atoms are far too small and fast to film, even with the best microscopes. So I simulate them on supercomputers.

Density functional theory (DFT)

Electrons are too small to watch, so the computer works out where they must be. It makes a guess, checks it against the laws of quantum physics, improves it, and repeats until the answer stops changing. Then we know the material's energy, its colour, and whether it conducts electricity.

Shown here: the holey C₂N monolayer, which I studied as a catalyst for lithium–air batteries (J. Mater. Chem. A 2025). DFT is the starting point of almost every project I've worked on.

Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD)

Atoms never sit still; they wiggle with heat. AIMD calculates the push on every atom from its electrons, moves each atom a tiny step, and repeats thousands of times. The result is a movie of the material with frames a millionth of a billionth of a second (a femtosecond) apart.

Shown here: the g-CN/WTe₂ heterostructure (J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 2022), a g-CN sheet resting on WTe₂, held only by weak van der Waals forces. Its AIMD runs later became training data for E(q)C-GNN.

Non-adiabatic molecular dynamics (NAMD)

When light hits a material it kicks an electron up to a high energy. Like a ball bouncing down stairs, the electron loses energy step by step, and that energy shakes the atoms as heat. NAMD measures how fast this happens. If it's too fast, solar energy is wasted as heat before it can be used.

The core of my Ph.D.: hot electrons in COFs and lead-free perovskites, and charge transfer between stacked 2D layers.

Surface hopping

Think of two hilly landscapes stacked on top of each other: an excited one and a calm one. After light hits, the atoms roll around on the upper landscape. Where the two landscapes nearly touch, the system can hop down. Surface hopping simulates many such journeys to predict how a material relaxes after absorbing light.

I run these simulations with Hefei-NAMD using several surface-hopping methods.

Machine learning: E(q)C-GNN

AIMD is accurate but painfully slow, because it solves DFT at every tiny time step. So we taught a neural network to do the job. We ran AIMD on real 2D materials, turned every snapshot into a graph where atoms are dots and bonds are lines, and let the network learn from nothing but the atoms' positions. Atoms pass messages to their neighbours, and the network predicts energies, entropy and forces within about 3% of AIMD. It also gives the same answer if you rotate the material, as physics demands, and it runs several orders of magnitude faster on an ordinary desktop computer.

D. Maji, A. Ghosh, D. Barman, P. Sarkar, J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 16, 2254–2264 (2025), tested on g-CN, WTe₂ and g-CN/WTe₂. Read the paper

Putting it all together: making ammonia on GaS

My favourite project uses every tool on this page to answer one question: can a thin sheet of gallium sulfide (GaS) turn nitrogen from the air into ammonia using only sunlight? DFT builds the sheet, AIMD checks it survives room temperature, and a graph neural network with "attention" shows where nitrogen likes to sit. Removing one sulfur atom exposes gallium atoms that grab N₂. Light then supplies electrons, every hydrogenation step becomes downhill, and NAMD shows the electrons last about 4 nanoseconds, thousands of times longer than in the common photocatalyst TiO₂, so they have time to react.

A. Ghosh et al., ACS Catal. 16, 3284–3295 (2026). Read the paper

What I use them for: clean-energy materials

The same tools answer very different questions, from solar power to fertiliser to batteries.

Solar cells (photovoltaics)

A solar cell turns light into electricity. Light knocks an electron loose, leaving a "hole" behind. The cell's layers push electrons one way and holes the other, so they flow through a wire and power a bulb.

2D hybrid perovskites for solar cells (J. Comput. Chem. 2021) and how defects affect lead-free tin perovskites (Nanoscale 2024).

Photocatalysis: fuel from sunlight and water

This is like artificial photosynthesis. A thin sheet of material soaks up sunlight and uses the energy to split water into hydrogen fuel and oxygen. For every bubble of oxygen you get two of hydrogen.

Stacked 2D photocatalysts such as MoS₂/BSe and Ti₂CO₂–WSe₂, Z-scheme designs, and covalent organic frameworks for overall water splitting.

Making fertiliser with sunlight: a boron-doped COF

Plants need ammonia, but making it today burns huge amounts of fossil fuel. We asked whether a metal-free porous sheet, an aza-COF, could do it with sunlight. Adding a single boron atom narrows the band gap from 1.97 to 1.53 eV, so the sheet soaks up more light. Light creates "hot" electrons that quickly cool to the band edge. Nitrogen from the air sticks to the boron end-on and receives electrons, which weakens its very strong triple bond. Hydrogen is then added one atom at a time until two ammonia molecules leave, and with the light's energy every step goes downhill. Finally, NAMD shows the electrons last about 2 picoseconds, longer than the ~0.5 ps they need to reach the nitrogen.

P. Das, A. Ghosh, P. Sarkar, J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 15, 4898–4905 (2024). Read the paper. Related: defective boron nitride (J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 2025) and the GaS study above.

Electrocatalysis

Electrocatalysis uses electricity, ideally from solar or wind, to drive chemical reactions such as splitting water. A good catalyst on the electrode makes the reaction faster and wastes less energy. I model what happens atom by atom at that surface to help design better catalysts.

My current work at UMass Amherst, including BiVO₄ surfaces for oxygen evolution with Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (J. Chem. Phys., in press).

Batteries

In a lithium-ion battery, lithium ions shuttle between two electrodes. When you use your phone they travel one way and push electrons through the circuit; charging sends them back. More "parking spots" for ions means a battery that stores more energy.

A holey carbon nitride anode with ultrahigh capacity (ChemPhysChem 2022), porous boron phosphide for Li and Na ions (J. Phys. Chem. C 2022), and a C₂N catalyst for Li–air batteries (J. Mater. Chem. A 2025).

Spintronics

Electrons spin like tiny magnets, pointing up or down. Ordinary electronics ignore this; spintronics uses it to store and send information with less power. Some materials act as a "half-metal": a one-way door that lets only one spin direction through.

My first papers: half-metallic vanadium nitride nanoribbons (PCCP 2021) and spin transport in CrC monolayers (Mater. Sci. Eng. B 2021).

Thermoelectrics: electricity from heat

Thermoelectric materials turn heat straight into electricity. Heat one end and charges drift toward the cold end, creating a voltage, with no moving parts. They could recover energy wasted as heat in car engines and factories.

Why the Zintl-phase compound KCaBi is so good at this (PCCP 2024).

Quantum dots and energy transfer

Quantum dots are crystals only a few nanometres wide, thousands of times thinner than a hair. They catch light and can pass its energy to a nearby dye molecule, like a relay race. Knowing how fast that hand-off happens helps build better light-harvesting devices and lighting.

At Wayne State: quantum-dot gel to dye energy transfer (J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 2026) and excited carriers in 2D perovskites (Nano Lett. and ACS Nano 2026).
